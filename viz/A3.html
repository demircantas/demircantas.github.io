Final Visualization Design

Design Process & Design Decisions

This visualization explores the relationship between weekly COVID-19 case counts and fatality severity over two distinct time periods: July 2020–June 2021 and July 2021–January 2022. The primary objective was to investigate seasonal trends, peak infection waves, and variations in the fatality rate, while also incorporating a personal timeline to contextualize broader patterns. A pseudo-prediction (we use ground truth from dataset in place of a prediction when data was not yet available) extending beyond the recorded dataset was introduced to depict potential post-January 2022 trends, differentiating observed case trajectories from expected developments. The visualization employs a two-chart layout to facilitate comparisons between different pandemic waves, emphasizing how infection surges and severity rates evolved over time.

The fatality severity index was computed as:

Severity Index = (New Deaths per Week) / (New Cases per Week)

Weekly aggregation was used to smooth short-term fluctuations and reduce statistical noise. The severity index values were then smoothed using a rolling average with a three-week window to further mitigate variability. This approach ensures that the index reflects broader trends rather than isolated anomalies. The visualization employs a color-encoded curvature comb to indicate week-to-week fluctuations in severity, while a red line plot tracks reported cases. The pseudo-prediction is visually distinguished through low-opacity styling to differentiate it from observed data. Annotations highlight key peaks and troughs, ensuring that critical moments in the pandemic's progression are clearly communicated.

Reflection

The iterative design process informed refinements to spacing, annotation clarity, and color usage. Early sketches emphasized the importance of comparing different waves of the pandemic, leading to the adoption of a split dual-chart layout. While the final design effectively highlights large-scale case trends and fatality severity, some trade-offs were made. For instance, within-week variations are downplayed due to weekly aggregation, and individual data points are not directly labeled to maintain clarity. Additionally, the pseudo-prediction line required careful balance to avoid overpowering actual case data while still illustrating projected trends. A finding that the visualization implies is that high case numbers are accompanied by low fatality rates. However, as the case number wave subsides, the fatality rate increases. Creating a pattern of high case numbers followed by high fatality rates. This pattern is not explicitly addressed in the visualization, but is observed through reflection.

One of the most challenging aspects was balancing narrative-driven elements with data integrity.

While working on this visualization, I realized that the timing of the prediction matches the dates when I lost my father, possibly due to a COVID-19 related illness. The assignment turned into an investigation of the timeline of my father's death which I did not face as directly before, with most uncertainties pertaining our loss still to be answered, let alone included in a dataset.

While the severity index conveys general trends, its computation assumes consistent case reporting, which may not fully capture underreported fatalities or shifts in testing practices. The process of critique and redesign helped refine the annotation positioning, the legend placement, and the overall readability of the visualization. While not all trade-offs could be resolved, the final visualization presents an analytical and structured view of the data, highlighting both the pandemic’s impact and the challenges inherent in visualizing epidemiological trends.
